## Part 1: The Biology and Physics of an ECG

### What exactly is a Heartbeat?
Every time your heart pumps blood, the muscle contraction is triggered by a tiny electrical shock generated by a natural pacemaker in your heart called the **Sinoatrial (SA) node**. 
An **Electrocardiogram (ECG or EKG)** is a machine that listens to these electrical shocks and records them as a graph.

A single, healthy heartbeat on an ECG has distinct waves:
1. **P Wave:** The upper chambers (atria) squeezing.
2. **QRS Complex:** The large, sharp spike when the lower chambers (ventricles) pump powerfully.
3. **T Wave:** The heart muscles relaxing and resetting for the next beat.

If an AI can learn the exact shape of a healthy P-QRS-T wave, it can spot diseases. For example, if the T wave is upside down, or the QRS spike is too wide, the AI knows something is wrong.

### The 12-Lead System (The 3D View)
You might wonder why our project code constantly references `dims=12` (dimensions = 12). 
This is because a standard medical ECG uses **12 Leads** (sensors).

Imagine taking a photograph of a car. If you only take a picture from the front, you might miss a huge dent on the back bumper. The heart is a 3D muscle, so doctors place 10 physical stickers on the patient's body (arms, legs, and 6 across the chest) to calculate 12 different electrical angles (leads):

* **Limb Leads (I, II, III, aVR, aVL, aVF):** These look at the heart vertically (up and down, left and right).
* **Chest/Precordial Leads (V1, V2, V3, V4, V5, V6):** These look at the heart horizontally (front to back).

**In Our Project:**
When the AI reads a single patient's 10-second recording, it is not looking at 1 squiggly line. It is looking at a massive matrix of numbers representing 12 squiggly lines happening simultaneously.

## Part 2: The PTB-XL Dataset (Our Training Data)

To teach our AI, we need a massive library of textbooks. We use the **PTB-XL** dataset.

### What makes PTB-XL special?
Before PTB-XL, most AI datasets only had a few hundred patients. PTB-XL contains **21,837 clinical 12-lead ECG records** from 18,885 patients (some patients had multiple visits). 

### How is the data stored?
If you look at the raw downloaded folder, you will see it is divided into subfolders (like `records100` and `records500`). 
Medical data isn't saved as `.png` images or `.mp4` videos. It is saved in the **WFDB (Waveform Database)** format. 

For every patient, there are two files:
1. **The `.hea` (Header) File:** A text file containing metadata.
   * Example: It tells us the patient's age, sex, the sampling rate (e.g., 500 Hz), and the baseline voltage measurements.
2. **The `.dat` (Data) File:** A raw binary file containing the actual voltage numbers. 

### The Sampling Rate (500 Hz)
In our code, you will see references to `fs=500` (sampling frequency = 500 Hz).
What does this mean? It means the ECG machine took a voltage measurement **500 times every single second**.

* Total time of the recording = 10 seconds.
* Measurements per second = 500.
* Total measurements per lead = `10 * 500 = 5000` numbers.

Because there are 12 leads, a single patient's raw data file is a massive spreadsheet of `5000 rows x 12 columns` (60,000 numbers for just one 10-second heartbeat!).

## Part 3: Project-Specific Code Examples

Let's look at how our project actually handles this data. 
In our dataset, every patient has a diagnosis assigned by a human cardiologist, stored in a giant spreadsheet called `ptbxl_database.csv`.

Here is an example of what that file looks like internally:

| ekg_id | patient_id | age | sex | scp_codes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 00001 | 15709 | 56 | 1 | `{'NORM': 100.0, 'LVOLT': 0.0, 'SR': 0.0}` |
| 00002 | 13243 | 19 | 0 | `{'NORM': 80.0, 'SBRAD': 0.0}` |
| 00003 | 20372 | 37 | 1 | `{'MI': 100.0}` |

**How our AI uses this:**
When we train our AI for Anomaly Detection, we *only* want to teach it what a healthy heart looks like. 
Our Python script (`preprocess.py`) reads this CSV file and searches the `scp_codes` column. It looks for the word `'NORM'` (Normal). 

If a patient has `'NORM'`, their 60,000 numbers are added to our `train.npy` training file. 
If a patient has `'MI'` (Myocardial Infarction / Heart Attack) or any other disease, they are excluded from the training set and saved for the final exam (`test.npy`).

### The Final Data Shape
By the end of our data extraction, we have **14,241** normal patients for training.
If you print the shape of our training data in Python (`print(train_data.shape)`), you get:
```python
(14241, 12, 4800)
```
**What do these numbers mean?**
* `14241`: The number of healthy patients we are studying.
* `12`: The number of electrical leads (sensors).
* `4800`: The number of time steps (our code crops the 5000 samples down to 4800 to make the math divide perfectly during the AI's neural network calculations).

This massive 3D cube of numbers is the exact textbook our TSRNet AI will read!
